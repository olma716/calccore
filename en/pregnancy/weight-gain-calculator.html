How much weight is normal to gain during pregnancy

Recommended weight gain depends on pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) — women with different starting weights have different energy reserves and risks. These guidelines were developed by the US Institute of Medicine (IOM) and are used by doctors worldwide as a primary reference point.

  • Underweight (BMI < 18.5): 12.5-18 kg (28-40 lb)
  • Normal weight (BMI 18.5-24.9): 11.5-16 kg (25-35 lb)
  • Overweight (BMI 25-29.9): 7-11.5 kg (15-25 lb)
  • Obese (BMI ≥30): 5-9 kg (11-20 lb)

For twin pregnancies, the recommended range is higher and depends on the same BMI category — the body needs to supply resources for two babies at once.

How weight gain is distributed across trimesters

  • First trimester (weeks 1-13): usually minimal gain, 0.5-2 kg for the whole trimester. Morning sickness often interferes with normal eating during this period, so small gains are normal.
  • Second trimester (weeks 14-27): pace accelerates to roughly 0.3-0.5 kg per week for women with normal BMI. Appetite usually improves along with growing fetal needs.
  • Third trimester (weeks 28-40): the fetus grows most actively, gain continues at a similar pace, sometimes slowing slightly in the final weeks.

What the gained weight consists of

Gained weight isn't just fat stores. An approximate breakdown at an average gain of 12.5 kg:

  • Baby: ~3.2-3.6 kg
  • Placenta: ~0.7 kg
  • Amniotic fluid: ~0.9 kg
  • Uterine enlargement: ~0.9 kg
  • Increased blood volume: ~1.4 kg
  • Breast tissue growth: ~0.4 kg
  • Fat stores and fluid: the rest

Why excess or insufficient weight gain matters

Too little weight gain is associated with a risk of low birth weight and preterm birth, and can also indicate insufficient maternal nutrition. Excessive gain increases the risk of gestational diabetes, preeclampsia (high blood pressure), delivery complications (including a higher likelihood of cesarean section), and longer postpartum recovery. In addition, excessive pregnancy weight gain is often linked to greater difficulty returning to pre-pregnancy weight afterward.

Practical tips for healthy weight gain

  • Weigh yourself regularly: measure weight at roughly the same time of day, in similar clothing, once a week — this makes it easier to track the real trend without random fluctuations.
  • Quality matters more than quantity: focus on nutrient density rather than just calories — protein, vegetables, whole grains, healthy fats.
  • Moderate physical activity: absent contraindications, light exercise (walking, swimming, prenatal yoga) helps manage the pace of weight gain.
  • Don't artificially restrict eating: pregnancy is not the time for weight-loss diets, even if you were overweight beforehand — your body needs resources to support the baby's development.

These guidelines are general reference points. Individual needs depend on health status, multiple pregnancy, and other factors — always consult the doctor managing your pregnancy, especially if you notice significant deviations from the recommended pace.

FAQ

Why does recommended weight gain depend on pre-pregnancy BMI?

Women who were underweight before pregnancy are recommended to gain more, while overweight or obese women are recommended to gain less, to minimize risks for both mother and baby.

What if I'm gaining weight faster or slower than recommended?

Minor deviations are normal. Significant differences from the recommended pace should be discussed with your doctor at a scheduled checkup.

Is weight gain evenly distributed throughout pregnancy?

No, gain in the first trimester is usually minimal (0.5-2 kg for the whole trimester), while the pace accelerates in the second and third trimesters.
