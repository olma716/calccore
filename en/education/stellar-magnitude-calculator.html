What the stellar magnitude calculator does

Four tabs cover the main problems of school and amateur astronomy. “Distance modulus” links the apparent magnitude m, the absolute magnitude M and the distance d. “Brightness ratio” shows how many times one star is brighter than another. “Luminosity from R and T” computes the luminosity from the radius and the temperature, and “Luminosity and M” converts luminosity to absolute magnitude and back.

Stellar magnitude

Stellar magnitude is a logarithmic brightness scale where a smaller number means a brighter star. The Sun has m = −26.74, the full Moon −12.7, Sirius −1.46, Vega 0.03, and the faintest naked-eye stars are about +6. The absolute magnitude M is the apparent magnitude a star would have at a distance of 10 parsecs: it is used to compare the true brightness of stars.

Distance modulus

The difference m − M = 5 · lg d − 5 (d in parsecs) is called the distance modulus. For Sirius (m = −1.46, d = 2.64 pc) we get M = −1.46 − 5 · lg 2.64 + 5 ≈ 1.43. Conversely, knowing m and M, the distance is d = 10^((m − M + 5) / 5) pc.

Pogson's law

A difference of 5 magnitudes corresponds to a brightness ratio of exactly 100, so one magnitude is 100^(1/5) ≈ 2.512 times. The flux ratio is F₁ / F₂ = 10^(0.4 · (m₂ − m₁)). The Sun is 14 magnitudes brighter than the full Moon, that is 10^5.6 ≈ 398107 times. Vega (0.03) is about 6 times brighter than Polaris (1.98).

Luminosity of a star

The luminosity (total radiated power) by the Stefan–Boltzmann law is L = 4πR²σT⁴. In solar units: L / L☉ = (R / R☉)² · (T / 5772 K)⁴. For Sirius A (R = 1.711 R☉, T = 9940 K) we get L ≈ 25.7 L☉. Luminosity and the absolute bolometric magnitude are related by L / L☉ = 10^(0.4 · (4.74 − Mbol)); M = −0.26 gives exactly 100 L☉.

Apparent magnitudes of well-known objects

The Sun has an apparent magnitude of −26.74, the full Moon about −12.7, Venus at its brightest up to −4.9, Sirius −1.46, Vega 0.03 and Polaris about 1.98. The limiting magnitude for the naked eye in a dark sky is about +6, for binoculars +9…+10, and for large telescopes much more. Each magnitude is a brightness difference of 2.512 times, so +6 stars are 100 times fainter than +1 stars.

Magnitudes and distances are approximate (catalogue data, rounded); the Sun's luminosity is the unit, T☉ = 5772 K, M☉bol = 4.74.

FAQ

Why do brighter stars have smaller magnitudes?

That is how the scale arose historically: Hipparchus divided the stars into 6 classes and the brightest were of the first magnitude. The modern scale is logarithmic and the brightest objects have negative values.

What is absolute magnitude?

The apparent magnitude a star would have at a distance of 10 parsecs from the observer.

What is the distance modulus?

The difference m − M. It depends only on the distance: m − M = 5 lg d − 5.

How many times brighter is a star by one magnitude?

By 100^(1/5) ≈ 2.512 times; by 5 magnitudes exactly 100 times.

How do I calculate the luminosity of a star?

L = R² · (T / 5772)⁴ in solar units, with R in solar radii and T in kelvins.

Why does temperature matter more than radius?

The luminosity is proportional to the fourth power of the temperature: doubling T gives 16 times more energy, while doubling R gives only 4 times.

What is the luminosity of the Sun?

L☉ = 3.828·10²⁶ W (the IAU nominal value) — the unit of luminosity.

What are the magnitudes of the Sun?

Apparent −26.74, absolute visual about 4.83, absolute bolometric 4.74.

What is bolometric magnitude?

A magnitude that accounts for all radiation of a star in all bands, not just visible light.

How accurate are the results?

The formulas are exact, but the catalogue values of m, d, R and T have uncertainties, so the answers are approximate, especially the logarithmic ones.

What is the faintest magnitude visible to the naked eye?

In a very dark sky about +6, in a city much less — only +3…+4.

Why is the absolute magnitude of the Sun 4.83?

That is the visual absolute magnitude: if the Sun were at a distance of 10 parsecs it would look like a star of about magnitude 4.83, barely noticeable.
