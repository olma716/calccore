What the calculator does

The calculator helps when you do not have the pan from the recipe: it compares the area of your pan with the original and tells you how many times more or less batter to use. If you also give the depth of the pans, a volume factor is worked out too. You can enter the sizes in centimeters or inches.

How the area factor is worked out

The area of a round pan is S = π × (diameter / 2)², of a square one S = side², of a rectangular one S = length × width, and of a ring (Bundt) S = π × ((D / 2)² − (d / 2)²), where D is the outer diameter and d the diameter of the hole. The factor is the area of your pan divided by the area of the recipe pan. For example a round pan of 9 inches has an area of 410 cm² and a square one of 8×8 inches 413 cm², so the factor is 1.01. The area factor keeps the batter at the same depth.

The volume factor and depth

The volume of a pan is its area times its depth. If the depths are the same, the area and volume factors are equal. If not, the volume factor shows how much the capacity differs. For the same batter depth use the area factor, and use the volume one only when you fill the pans to the same fraction of their depth. For every pan the calculator also shows the range of batter at ½–⅔ full and the maximum at ¾.

How full to fill a pan

Food Network advises filling a pan about halfway to two-thirds, and a reply on the King Arthur site no more than three-quarters, because recipes rise differently. So the calculator shows both: the ½–⅔ range and the ¾ maximum.

Baking time

Baking time does not multiply by the factor. In a smaller, deeper pan the batter is thicker and takes longer, perhaps at a slightly lower temperature, in a larger, shallower one it takes less. Check with a skewer or a toothpick, not only by the timer. If the batter in the new pan has the same depth as in the original, the baking time is close to the one given.

The equivalent pan finder

The “Find a pan” tab shows sizes of the same area for the recipe pan: the diameter of a round pan, the side of a square one, the length of rectangular pans for typical widths and the three nearest standard pans. For example a 9-inch round pan is roughly an 8-inch square one.

Standard pans

The “Standard pans” tab shows the area of 17 common pans: round 8, 9 and 10 inches, square 8×8 and 9×9 inches, rectangular 9×13 inches, a 9×5 inch loaf, and also round 18–28 cm, square 20, 22 and 24 cm and rectangular 20×30 cm. The sizes are nominal: real pans can differ by a centimeter or two. A 9×13 inch rectangular pan is about 1.8 times as large as an 8×8 inch square one.

Limits

The calculator does not know how a recipe reacts to a change of pan: cakes, sponges, brownies and muffins behave differently. For very deep pans and pans with a hole the volume is more exact. Check the result by the state of the batter and the bake.

The area factor keeps the batter at the same depth. Baking time does not multiply by the factor: in a deeper pan the batter takes longer, in a shallower one less, so check with a skewer. The sizes of the standard pans are nominal.

FAQ

How do I swap a baking pan?

Work out the area of both pans and divide the area of yours by the area of the recipe pan: that is the factor for the batter. The calculator does it automatically.

How do I work out the area of a round pan?

S = π × (diameter / 2)². For a 9-inch pan that is 410 cm².

Can I use a square pan instead of a round one?

Yes, if the area is close. A 9-inch round pan is roughly an 8-inch square one, so an 8×8 inch pan almost fits (factor 1.01).

How much larger is a 9×13 inch pan than an 8×8?

About 1.8 times: 755 cm² against 413 cm².

Do I need to change the baking time?

Yes, the time does not multiply by the factor. A deeper, smaller pan takes longer, a shallower, larger one less. Check with a skewer.

Which is better for swapping: area or volume?

For the same batter depth, area. Volume is needed when pans of different depth are filled to the same fraction.

How full should I fill a pan?

Food Network advises halfway to two-thirds, and a reply on the King Arthur site no more than three-quarters. Recipes rise differently.

How do I swap a Bundt pan?

Enter it as a ring: the outer diameter and the diameter of the hole. For accuracy give the depth too so the volume is worked out.

How do I scale the ingredients to the new factor?

Use the link under the result: it opens the recipe scaler with the factor already entered.

Can I enter sizes in inches?

Yes, choose the units “Inches”: the results show both cm² and in².

What are the standard pan sizes?

The most common are round pans of 8–10 inches or 18–28 cm, square ones of 8×8 and 9×9 inches or 20–24 cm, and a 9×13 inch rectangular one. The sizes are nominal.

Does the calculator take the height of the sides into account?

Only when you give the depth: then the volume of the pan and the range of batter are worked out too.
