How much water you need a day

There is no single exact norm: the need depends on weight, activity, climate and health. Official guides give total water from drinks and food: EFSA (EU) names 2.5 L a day for men and 2.0 L for women, and the US National Academies 3.7 and 2.7 L. From drinks alone this is about 2.0 and 1.6 L by EFSA and 3.0 and 2.2 L by the American values, because about 20% of water comes from food. These figures are for adults at moderate temperature and activity.

How the calculator works

The base formula is simple: weight × 33 ml, then adjustments. For a 75 kg person with moderate activity (+350 ml) that is 75 × 33 + 350 = 2825 ml, i.e. 2.83 L, or about 11 glasses of 250 ml. The “30–35 ml per kg” rule is very common but has no official source, so the calculator lets you change the factor from 20 to 50 ml per kg and shows the official reference values alongside for comparison.

Adjustments for activity, heat and special conditions

On top of the usual load 0, 200, 350 or 700 ml is added depending on whether you have a desk job with little movement, a normal day with walks but no workouts, work out 3–5 times a week or train intensely every day; in hot weather another +500 ml. These are guides with no official source, not a clinical norm. For pregnancy EFSA suggests adding 300 ml a day, for breastfeeding about 700 ml. If you sweat a lot, the sweat rate tab will show your need more precisely.

Sweat rate during exercise

Weigh yourself before and after exercise. Sweat = weight lost + fluid drunk − urine, if you went to the toilet. Example: 70 kg before, 69.4 kg after, 400 ml drunk in 60 minutes: a loss of 600 ml + 400 ml drunk = 1000 ml of sweat, i.e. 1000 ml per hour. The weight loss is 0.9% of body mass. By the position of the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) you should aim not to lose more than 2% of body mass during exercise and to replace about 150% of the weight lost afterwards (1.5 L per kg): in the example about 900 ml over a few hours. Sweat rate is very individual, usually roughly from 0.4 to 1.8 L per hour depending on the person, heat and effort, so it is best measured yourself. Weigh in the same dry clothes or without them: wet clothing holds some sweat and understates the loss.

A drinking schedule by hours

The “Daily schedule” tab spreads the amount evenly from waking up to an hour before bed. For 2825 ml with waking at 07:00 and bed at 23:00 that is 11 drinks of about 257 ml every 1 hour 30 minutes, the last at 22:00. The schedule works as a reminder: drinking small portions regularly is easier than “catching up” in the evening. A last drink an hour before bed helps you wake less at night.

What counts towards the amount

Total water intake includes not only water but also tea, coffee, juices, soups and the water in food: watermelon, cucumbers and oranges contain up to 90–95% water. Caffeine has a mild diuretic effect, but drinks with it still count. The colour of urine is a handy guide: dark yellow means you should drink more, pale yellow or clear means you have enough.

When water harms

Too much water in a short time is dangerous: dilution of blood sodium (hyponatraemia) can occur with very large amounts of drinking, especially during long exercise. That is why the calculator warns about values over 5 litres a day. People with heart or kidney disease, on fluid restriction or pregnant should have the amount set by a doctor.

This is a guide for a healthy adult, not medical advice: with heart or kidney disease, fluid restriction, pregnancy or medication the amount is set by a doctor.

FAQ

How much water should I drink a day?

It depends on weight, activity and climate. The calculator's guide is 33 ml per kg plus adjustments; for a 75 kg person with moderate activity that is about 2.8 litres.

Is it true that I need 2 litres?

It is only a guide. EFSA names 2.0 L of total water for women and 2.5 L for men, including water from food, and the need for drinks depends on the person.

Do tea, coffee and soup count?

Yes, all fluids and the water in food count towards the total balance. Caffeine has a mild diuretic effect, but tea and coffee still add fluid.

Where does 33 ml per kg come from?

It is a common but unofficial rule of thumb. The calculator lets you change the factor and shows the official EFSA and US National Academies values for comparison.

How much should I drink in pregnancy and while breastfeeding?

EFSA suggests adding about 300 ml a day in pregnancy and about 700 ml while breastfeeding. Agree the exact amount with a doctor.

How do I calculate fluid loss during exercise?

Weigh yourself before and after. Sweat = weight lost + fluid drunk − urine; divide by the duration in hours to get the sweat rate.

How much should I drink after exercise?

About 150% of the weight lost, i.e. 1.5 L for each kilogram, over a few hours (an ACSM recommendation).

Why is losing more than 2% of body weight a problem?

Losing more than 2% of body mass through sweat reduces endurance and performance. So during exercise you should drink to avoid reaching that level.

How do I spread water through the day?

Evenly from waking up to an hour before bed. The calculator builds a schedule from your hours and glass size.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes. Very large amounts in a short time can dilute blood sodium (hyponatraemia). Do not drink more than ~5 litres a day without need.

How do I know I drink enough?

Pale yellow or clear urine usually means good hydration, dark yellow means you should drink more.

Does the amount depend on climate?

Yes. In heat and with heavy sweating the need grows; the calculator adds about 500 ml for a hot climate.
