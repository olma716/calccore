What the calculator does

The calculator rescales a recipe to a different number of servings. You paste the ingredients, one per line, say how many servings the recipe makes and how many you need, and get a new list with the amounts recalculated. It helps when a recipe is written for 4 people and you have 6 or 10 guests, or the other way round when you need a single portion of a big recipe. You can copy the scaled lines or share a link to the recipe with the chosen factor.

How to scale a recipe by hand

First find the factor: divide the servings you need by the servings in the recipe. For 6 servings instead of 4 it is 6 / 4 = 1.5. Then multiply every amount by the factor: 200 g of flour becomes 300 g, 1 1/2 cups of flour becomes 2 ¼ cups and 3 eggs become 4.5 eggs. The calculator does it automatically and rounds the result to a practical precision.

Three ways to scale

The “By servings” tab works out the factor from two numbers: the servings in the recipe and the servings you need. The “By factor” tab lets you set the multiplier directly, for example ×2, ½ or 1 1/2, with quick buttons for ¼, ½, ¾, ×2, ×3 and ×4. The “From what you have” tab answers “I only have 150 g of flour, what do I get?”: choose a line of the recipe, enter how much of that ingredient you have and the rest is scaled in proportion.

Which entries the calculator understands

An amount can stand at the start of a line: “2 cups flour”, “1/2 cup sugar”, “1 1/2 cups”, “½ tsp”, “0.5 l milk” or “250g butter”. Ranges are supported (“2-3 cloves garlic”, “250 to 300 g flour”) and are scaled at both ends. The amount can also come after the name: “Flour — 200 g”, “Flour: 2 cups”, “Eggs 3”. Lines with list markers (a dash, a bullet, numbering) are fine too. Numbers in brackets such as “can (14 oz)” scale together with the amount. Lines without a number (“salt to taste”) and amounts in minutes or degrees stay unchanged.

Correct word forms

When an amount changes, unit words get the right form: 1 cup, 2 cups; 1 egg, 3 eggs; 1 clove, 4 cloves. This works for known words: cup, tablespoon, teaspoon, egg, clove, can, pinch, piece, slice, stick, onion, tomato, lemon and others. Other words in the line stay as you typed them. Abbreviations (g, ml, tbsp, tsp) do not change.

Hints for amounts

Next to an amount the calculator shows a handier form without changing the result: 1500 g = 1.5 kg, 4 tbsp ≈ ¼ cup, 0.67 cup ≈ ⅔, 6 tsp = 2 tbsp. You can choose the cup in the settings: 250 or 200 ml, the US 240 ml or the British 284 ml. To convert cups and spoons to grams by ingredient there is the cooking measurement converter.

What does not scale in direct proportion

The main ingredients scale linearly, but part of a recipe does not. Spices and salt are better added gradually with tasting. Baking powder and soda taste bitter in large amounts, and yeast for a bigger dough usually needs less than direct proportion. Cooking time does not multiply by the factor: check for doneness at the usual time. Pan and dish size should change with the amount so that the layer does not become too thin or too thick (pan size converter). Liquid evaporates faster in a wider pan. The calculator notices such lines and points it out when the factor is 2 or more, or 0.5 or less.

Eggs and counted items

If scaling gives 4.5 eggs or 1.5 onions, the calculator shows the exact number and a hint: an egg does not divide, so beat it and use the part you need, while other counted items are usually rounded to a whole number. Pay attention to this first for small factors such as ½.

Limits

One line is read as one ingredient: if a line has several amounts, the first is scaled together with the numbers in brackets next to units of weight and volume. A recipe can have up to 200 lines. A share link holds up to 800 characters of the recipe.

The calculator scales amounts linearly. Spices, salt, leavening, yeast, cooking time and pan size should be adjusted to taste and to how the dish looks: they do not grow in direct proportion.

FAQ

How do I scale a recipe to a different number of servings?

Divide the servings you need by the servings in the recipe and multiply every amount by that factor. For 6 servings instead of 4 the factor is 1.5. The calculator does it for the whole list at once.

How do I double a recipe?

On the “By factor” tab enter 2 or press ×2. Every amount doubles, but cooking time, spices and leavening are better adjusted separately.

How do I halve a recipe?

Enter the factor ½ or 1/2. For eggs the calculator will remind you that half an egg has to be beaten and divided.

Can I enter fractions?

Yes: 1/2, 1 1/2, 1-1/2, ½, 1½ and decimals with a comma or a point. Ranges such as 2-3 are supported too.

What if I only have part of an ingredient?

Use the “From what you have” tab: choose the line, enter how much of that ingredient you have and the calculator scales the rest in proportion.

Does cooking time multiply by the factor?

No. A larger portion or pan takes longer, but not in direct proportion, so start checking at the usual time and watch the dish.

Should spices and salt scale the same way?

It is better to add them gradually and taste: in large portions flavor grows more slowly than the amount, and in small ones they are hard to measure, so take a pinch and taste.

What do I do when scaling gives half an egg?

Beat the egg, divide it and use the part you need. Sometimes the recipe can be changed so that the eggs come out whole, but that depends on the dish.

Do word forms like “cup” and “cups” change?

Yes, for known words (cup, egg, clove, can and others). Other words stay as in your line.

Can I write “Flour — 200 g” instead of “200 g flour”?

Yes, the amount may come after a dash, a colon or simply after the name: “Flour — 200 g”, “Flour: 2 cups”, “Eggs 3”.

Are numbers in brackets, such as “can (14 oz)”, scaled?

Yes, numbers next to units of weight and volume in brackets scale together with the main amount. Temperatures and times in brackets do not change.

Do I need to change the baking pan?

Yes, the area of the pan should change roughly with the amount of dough. Use the pan size converter to work it out.
