              How braking distance is calculated

              A full stop has two parts. The reaction distance is speed × driver reaction time: the car keeps moving before the driver starts braking. The braking distance is v² ÷ (2a), where a = g × μ is the deceleration and μ is the friction coefficient between the tires and the road. For 100 km/h, a 1 s reaction and dry asphalt: 27.8 m of reaction + 46.3 m of braking = 74.0 m.

              Speed is squared in the formula, so doubling the speed makes the braking distance four times longer: 11.6 m at 50 km/h and 46.3 m at 100 km/h.

              Friction μ: surface, tires and temperature

              The main thing that changes the braking distance is μ. The calculator uses typical values: dry road 0.85, wet 0.60, packed snow 0.30 and ice 0.12 for winter tires, and then multiplies them by a tire factor. On dry and wet roads summer and worn tires lose grip in the cold and winter tires lose grip in the heat. On snow and ice the tires differ much more: in a Swiss test from 50 km/h winter tires stopped on snow in 32 m and summer tires in 63 m; in an ice test studded tires brake noticeably better than non-studded ones.

              Road slope and an obstacle

              A downhill slope lengthens the braking distance and an uphill slope shortens it: the model uses a = g × (μ·cosθ + sinθ). For example, on dry asphalt from 100 km/h a 10% downhill gives 52.7 m of braking instead of 46.3 m. If the grip on a steep downhill is too low, the car will not stop at all — the calculator shows that.

              Enter the distance to an obstacle and the calculator checks whether the road is long enough. If not, it calculates the impact speed: on a wet road from 100 km/h with an obstacle 60 m ahead (1 s reaction) the car reaches it at about 71 km/h.

              The result is an estimate from typical friction values: real distances depend on ABS, brakes, tire pressure, load and the state of the road. Do not rely on the calculator for safety-critical decisions.

              FAQ

              What does braking distance depend on?

              Most of all on speed (the distance grows with its square), then on tire grip on the road (surface, tire type and condition, temperature) and on the slope. The driver’s reaction time is added as a separate part of the full stopping distance.

              Why does doubling the speed make the braking distance four times longer?

              Because kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, while the braking force is limited by grip. On dry asphalt the braking distance is 11.6 m at 50 km/h and 46.3 m at 100 km/h.

              What is the difference between braking distance and total stopping distance?

              Braking distance is the distance from the start of braking to a stop. The total stopping distance also includes the reaction distance. For 100 km/h and a 1 s reaction on dry asphalt it is 46.3 m + 27.8 m = 74.0 m.

              How many metres does a car travel during the reaction time?

              The speed in m/s multiplied by the time. At 100 km/h that is 27.8 m for every second; with a 2 s reaction (distracted) it is 55.6 m before braking even starts.

              How much longer is braking on a wet road?

              With typical values about 42% longer: 65.5 m instead of 46.3 m on dry asphalt from 100 km/h. With worn tires the difference is bigger.

              How much better are winter tires than summer tires on snow?

              In the calculator summer tires have half the grip of winter tires on packed snow: from 100 km/h the braking distance is 262 m versus 131 m. In the Swiss test from 50 km/h it was 63 m versus 32 m.

              Do studs help?

              Mostly on ice: in the calculator studded tires have about 45% more grip on ice than non-studded winter tires (braking distance from 100 km/h 226 m versus 328 m). On snow the difference is small.

              How does the road slope affect braking?

              The braking distance is longer downhill and shorter uphill. On dry asphalt from 100 km/h a 10% downhill increases braking from 46.3 m to 52.7 m.

              Does the braking distance depend on the weight of the car?

              In the simplified model the mass cancels out, so the distance does not depend on it. In reality heavier cars brake worse because of brake heat and tire load, but speed and grip are decisive.

              Does the calculator take ABS into account?

              The model assumes optimal braking, close to what ABS does. If the wheels lock, grip is worse and the braking distance longer, especially on wet and slippery surfaces.

              What is the impact speed?

              If the obstacle is closer than the full stopping distance, the car hits it at some speed: the calculator subtracts the braking work over the remaining distance from the kinetic energy. For 100 km/h on a wet road with an obstacle 60 m ahead it is about 71 km/h.

              How accurate is the model?

              It is a simplified estimate from typical friction values. Real distances depend on ABS, the state of brakes and tires, pressure, load and the surface. Always keep a safety margin.
