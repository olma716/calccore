Why you need a cooking thermometer

Visual cues (juice color, meat color) are unreliable for determining food safety. The only accurate way to ensure meat is safely cooked is to measure the internal temperature with a cooking thermometer.

How to measure temperature correctly

Insert the thermometer into the thickest part of the food, avoiding bones, fat, and cartilage (they conduct heat differently and give inaccurate readings). For poultry, the best spot is the inner thigh, without touching the bone. For large cuts (roast beef, whole turkey), check temperature at multiple points to ensure even doneness.

What "resting" meat means and why it matters

Resting meat is the period after removing a dish from heat during which it's left uncut, allowing the fibers to "settle." This happens for two reasons:

  • Carryover cooking: the outer layers of meat are hotter than the center, and that heat continues transferring inward even after removal from heat. The center temperature can rise another 3-8°C depending on the size of the cut — so large pieces (turkey, roast beef) should be removed from heat a few degrees before the target temperature.
  • Juice redistribution: during cooking, heat pushes juices toward the center of the cut. If you cut into meat immediately, most of the juice will run out onto the plate or cutting board. After a few minutes of resting, the muscle fibers relax and reabsorb the juices evenly throughout the piece — which is why rested meat stays juicy rather than dry when sliced.

How long to rest different types of meat

  • Small cuts (chicken breasts, steaks, pork chops): 3-5 minutes is enough.
  • Medium cuts (roast chicken, small roast beef): 10-15 minutes.
  • Large cuts (whole turkey, large roast): 20-30 minutes, tented with foil to retain heat.

Rule of thumb: the larger the cut of meat, the longer it should rest — roughly 1 minute of resting per 100-150g of weight for large cuts.

How to use this guide in practice

  1. Choose a food category (poultry, beef/lamb, pork, fish, eggs/leftovers) from the list above.
  2. Find the specific dish and desired doneness level in the table.
  3. Check the temperature with a thermometer in the thickest part about 5-10 minutes before expected doneness.
  4. Remove from heat slightly before the target temperature (accounting for carryover), then rest for the recommended time.
  5. Slice and serve — the juices will stay inside the meat rather than on the cutting board.

Why different foods have different requirements

Poultry (chicken, turkey) requires a higher temperature (74°C/165°F) due to increased salmonella risk. Beef and lamb can be safely eaten at lower temperatures (medium rare, 63°C/145°F), since bacteria are usually only on the surface and are killed during searing. Ground meat always needs full cooking, because bacteria can be mixed throughout the volume during grinding.

Temperature values are based on general food safety guidelines. For immunocompromised individuals, pregnant women, and young children, choosing a higher temperature threshold is recommended.

FAQ

Why is it important to let meat rest after cooking?

During resting, juices redistribute throughout the meat, making it juicier. The temperature also continues to rise slightly (carryover cooking).

Why is the temperature for chicken higher than for beef?

Poultry has a higher risk of salmonella contamination, requiring full cooking to 74°C, while beef can be safely eaten at lower temperatures (medium rare).

Can I rely on the color of meat alone to determine doneness?

No, color is an unreliable indicator — the only accurate way to check meat safety is a cooking thermometer inserted into the thickest part, away from bone.
